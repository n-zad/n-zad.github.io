# Portfolio Website Content

Build a personal home for the owner's work, curiosity, and interests that also helps employers assess their capabilities. Give visitors useful evidence and reasons to remember the person. Complement the resume and LinkedIn with motivation, decisions, artifacts, and personality rather than reproducing their full contents.

## Voice and positioning

- Write in a friendly, direct first-person voice. Use natural contractions and occasional light humor when supported by the owner's wording or interests.
- Express confidence through clear ownership and concrete work. Avoid inflated expertise, corporate slogans, generic passion statements, and apologetic phrases such as "just a student" or "only a side project."
- Identify the owner's current studies and professional direction succinctly. Do not imply that a student is a senior engineer, established researcher, or agency.
- Preserve how the owner actually talks. Do not invent jokes, quirks, personal history, or a branded persona to make the website memorable.
- Keep casual language understandable to visitors outside a hobby or technical niche. Use straightforward section names and action labels.
- Avoid em dashes in drafted copy. Prefer short sentences over dense paragraphs or strings of role and technology keywords.

## Information architecture

Support two levels of exploration: a quick visit should reveal identity, direction, strong work, and contact; a longer visit should reveal how the owner thinks and what they enjoy making or collecting.

Include or deliberately account for:

- **Introduction:** name, a short description of current work or studies, and a specific interest or motivation when known. Avoid duplicating the same biography in the hero and About section.
- **Selected work:** a few strong, representative projects. Start with roughly three to five if enough suitable work exists; choose based on available evidence, relevance, originality, and personal ownership.
- **More work:** an optional compact collection of additional projects, experiments, hackathon builds, or ongoing work. Allow breadth when entries are meaningful; do not make every project a full case study.
- **About:** useful personal context, motivations, working interests, and a small amount of background. Let the resume carry detailed chronology.
- **Personal interests or collections:** a discoverable place for genuine interests, including music playlists or hobby tools when the owner wants them included.
- **Professional context:** concise education, experience, research, or recognition where it adds information needed to assess the work. Separate sections are optional, not mandatory.
- **Contact and external links:** a clear way to reach the owner, plus relevant GitHub, LinkedIn, resume, demo, or publication links.

Combine sections when that makes the site clearer. Do not create a full resume timeline, coursework catalog, blog, testimonials, or other template sections without worthwhile material. Treat this as a flexible structure, not a required page order.

## Project selection and depth

- Feature work that demonstrates a meaningful question, useful tool, interesting decision, or distinctive personal motivation. Small finished tools can deserve prominence alongside academic and research projects.
- Do not exclude a project merely because it involves a game, a hobby, or AI-assisted development. Evaluate what it does, what the owner contributed, and what can be inspected.
- Use an AI-assisted Clash Royale deck-management PWA as an example of a personal tool: explain the actual need and demonstrated behavior without recasting it as an enterprise product.
- Include ongoing projects when there is something substantive to show. Label their status clearly and distinguish demonstrated results from planned features.
- Keep featured summaries brief: what it is, why it matters or why the owner made it, one meaningful contribution or result, and a relevant link. Add only a small number of useful technology labels.
- Offer deeper explanations where enough material exists. Do not require a long case study for every experiment, playlist, or small utility.

For a detailed project page, capture what is available:

1. The problem, research question, or personal motivation.
2. What the project does, supported by an actual screenshot, demo, output, or other artifact.
3. The owner's contribution, with clear attribution for team work.
4. Important technical, product, or research decisions and their rationale.
5. A meaningful challenge or tradeoff, and how it was handled.
6. Results, findings, current limitations, completion status, or lessons.
7. Links that let visitors inspect the work further.

Scale detail to the project. Use measured outcomes when verified; otherwise use concrete behavior or findings. Do not manufacture users, impact, or impressive numbers to justify inclusion.

## AI-assisted work and attribution

- Describe the owner's actual role in requirements, implementation, testing, evaluation, or iteration only where supported by evidence.
- Acknowledge substantial AI assistance plainly when discussing how the work was built, especially if omitting it would overstate individual authorship. A brief note is usually enough; it need not dominate the project summary.
- Use "AI-assisted" as an accessible description when accurate. Retain "vibe-coded" if it fits the owner's voice, with enough context for visitors to understand the work.
- Distinguish using AI to build software from building an AI system. Do not label an ordinary app an AI project solely because a coding assistant helped create it.
- Attribute collaborators and external foundations where relevant. Avoid implying sole ownership of team achievements or generated work.

## Personal interests and collections

- Include interests because they help visitors know the person. Do not force every hobby to prove employability, leadership, or a technical skill.
- Give a curated YouTube playlist a short explanation of its theme, mood, or why the owner assembled it, plus a clear link. Do not invent genres, audience size, or a curation philosophy.
- Prefer a few meaningful collections or details over an unfiltered list of every interest or account.
- Let hobby software appear in Selected Work when it has a strong story; other interests can live in an optional section such as "Elsewhere" or "Outside of code."
- Avoid duplicating a project's full description across professional and personal sections. Use a cross-link when it belongs in both contexts.
- Keep these areas available to browse while ensuring a visitor can still assess the strongest work quickly.

## Evidence and accuracy

- Demonstrate skills through projects and experience. Add a compact technology summary only when it helps visitors scan; avoid proficiency bars, percentages, and unsupported expertise labels.
- Never invent facts, technologies, employers, dates, metrics, awards, responsibilities, or outcomes. Ask for essential missing information; use clearly marked placeholders only in drafts, and remove or resolve them before publishing.
- Link claims to inspectable artifacts when possible. Do not claim that a demo, repository, resume, or contact method exists or works without checking it.
- Use project names or a personal handle consistently when supplied. Keep the person's identity clear and avoid manufacturing an agency-like brand.
- Favor specific details over self-evaluative adjectives. Remove copy that could describe almost any student without modification.

## Final content check

Before finalizing, confirm that visitors can quickly identify the owner, their direction, their strongest work, their individual contributions, and a way to contact them or view a resume. Confirm that deeper exploration reveals real motivations, decisions, or interests beyond the resume, with no forced professional justification for personal content.

Use `portfolio-ui-ux` for visual style, layout execution, and interaction behavior. Own content hierarchy and tone here without prescribing fonts, colors, animation, or component geometry.
